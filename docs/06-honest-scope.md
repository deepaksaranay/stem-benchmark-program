# How to describe this project honestly

This is a **self-built practice project with synthetic data**. It shows that you understand how a STEM AI
evaluation program is designed and measured. It does **not** show that you ran one for real.

## What it proves
- You can design verifiable STEM tasks, with two independent solutions and automatic grading.
- You can write a safe grader that handles units, tolerances and hostile input.
- You can define program metrics precisely, compute them correctly and read them (the demo data shows a
  slow reviewer and a low-quality contributor, and the report says what to do about each).
- You can turn a customer request into a plan, a rubric, a risk register and a delivery report.
- You can put it all under tests and CI.

## What it does NOT prove
| A recruiter may ask | This project |
|---|---|
| Led a program from customer request to delivery? | Shows the *method*. Not a real delivery |
| Managed a team of contributors or reviewers? | **No.** There was no team |
| Owned real production numbers? | **No.** The numbers are synthetic |
| Handled customer escalations and budgets? | **No.** The charter is a template |

## Good ways to say it
- "I built a simulation of an evaluation program to practice the operating model. The data is synthetic, and the
  repository says so on the first page."
- "The grader catches wrong units and hostile input. I tested it, and here is the test suite."

## Things never to say
- Do not present the demo numbers as results of real work.
- Do not claim you managed people or customers because you wrote a RACI table.

## What would make the experience real
- Take real ownership of something small: a study group, an open-source benchmark, a volunteer review
  team. Keep honest records of the real numbers.
- Ask to review other people's tasks and track your own review time and accuracy.
- Aim first for roles that match what you have done: STEM evaluator, benchmark engineer, AI quality specialist.
  A program-lead role is a later step.
